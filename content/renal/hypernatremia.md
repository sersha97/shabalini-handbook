:::bottom
- **Na >145 = too little water for the salt.** It is a **water** problem, not a salt problem.
- **Shock or very dry?** Give **LR or NS first** to fix the volume. Then give **water**.
- **Give water:** **free-water flushes** if the gut works, or **D5W IV**. NS is **not** water.
- **Speed:** chronic or unknown → lower Na by **about 10 mEq/L per 24 h**. Acute (<48 h) → **1 mEq/L per hour** back to normal.
- **Check Na every 4–6 h** (every 2–4 h if acute or Na >165). Add **ongoing losses** to the plan.
- **Big volume of dilute urine (U-osm <300)?** Think **diabetes insipidus** → give **desmopressin**.
- Most hospital cases are **made by us**: NPO, no water ordered, diuretics, lactulose, tube feeds, fever.
:::

## 🧠 Pathophysiology in 60 seconds

- Two defenses keep Na normal: **thirst** (drink) and **ADH** (kidney saves water).
- Hypernatremia happens when **one defense fails and the patient cannot drink**. The thirsty patient who can reach a cup does not get hypernatremia.
- Who cannot drink? **Old, confused, intubated, sedated, NPO, babies.**
- High Na pulls **water out of cells**. The brain **shrinks**. Veins between brain and skull can **tear** → bleed.
- After **~48 h** the brain makes its own osmolytes and goes back to near-normal size. Now, too much water too fast can cause **swelling**.

{{fig:raisin-brain}}

:::analogy
Think of the patient as a **bathtub** of salty water. The salt stays the same. The water level is low. The **faucet** is thirst and drinking. The **drain** is the kidney, the gut, the skin and the lungs. Your job: **open the faucet** (give water) and **close the drain** (fix the cause).
:::

### Symptoms

- **Thirst** first — but old and confused patients often do not feel it or cannot say it.
- Then **lethargy, weakness, irritability, confusion**, muscle twitching, hyperreflexia.
- Severe (usually Na >160): **seizures, coma**, intracranial **bleed**.
- High Na is a marker of **high mortality** — most of the risk comes from the illness under it.

## 🔎 Causes

:::mnemonic The 5 D's
- **D**on't drink — no thirst, no access (dementia, intubated, NPO, stroke)
- **D**rained — water lost from **skin** (fever, sweat, burns), **gut** (diarrhea, **lactulose**, NG output), **lungs** (fast breathing)
- **D**iabetes insipidus — central or nephrogenic
- **D**iuresis — **osmotic** (glucose, urea, mannitol) or **loop diuretic**
- **D**octor-made — hypertonic saline, sodium bicarbonate, lots of NS, salt tabs
:::

| Volume status | Typical causes |
|---|---|
| **Low** (most common) | GI loss, skin loss, osmotic diuresis, loop diuretic + no water |
| **Normal** | DI, insensible loss, no water access |
| **High** | NS or bicarb load in ICU, hypertonic saline, hypertonic feeds, (rare) hyperaldosteronism |

:::mnemonic Nephrogenic DI — the kidney got a "LiCK"
**Li**thium · high **Ca** · low **K**. Also: post-obstruction, recovering ATN, demeclocycline, amphotericin B, pregnancy (vasopressinase).
:::

## 🧪 Diagnosis

- **Labs:** BMP, **glucose**, **BUN** (urea), **Ca**, Mg, serum osm.
- **Urine:** **urine osm**, urine Na + K, **urine volume** (strict I/O). Weight.
- **Look at the MAR and the I/O:** lactulose, furosemide, tube-feed type, mannitol, hypertonic saline, fever curve.

{{fig:hyperna-kidney}}

:::pearl Patient already hypernatremic? Skip the water-deprivation test
The patient is already "deprived." Give **desmopressin 1–2 mcg IV/SC** and measure **urine osm in 1–2 h**. Big rise = **central**. No rise = **nephrogenic**. A **copeptin** level >21 pmol/L (no water deprivation) also points to **nephrogenic** DI.
:::

:::new New names (2022)
Central DI → **arginine vasopressin deficiency (AVP-D)**. Nephrogenic DI → **arginine vasopressin resistance (AVP-R)**. Same disease, new name — do not get fooled on the boards.
:::

## 💊 Management

{{fig:water-mnemonic}}

:::flow
Q: Shock, low BP or clearly very dry?
Y: Give LR or NS boluses first. Volume before water.
Q: Did the Na rise in less than 48 h (salt load, DI after pituitary surgery)?
Y: Acute → lower Na 1 mEq/L per hour to normal. Consider dialysis for massive salt poisoning.
N: Chronic or unknown → lower Na about 10 mEq/L per 24 h. Do not let it stall.
Q: Gut works and the patient has a tube or can drink?
Y: Enteral free water (flushes or by mouth). Put water within reach.
N: D5W IV. Check glucose.
Q: Urine still big and dilute (U-osm <300)?
Y: Desmopressin (central DI) or fix the cause (nephrogenic DI).
:::

:::tabs
=== Calculate
**1. Total body water (TBW) = weight (kg) × factor**

| Patient | Factor |
|---|---|
| Young man | 0.6 |
| Young woman / old man | 0.5 |
| Old woman | 0.45 |

**2. Free water deficit (FWD) = TBW × (Na ÷ 140 − 1)**

**3. Effect of 1 L of fluid on Na (Adrogué–Madias):**
ΔNa = (infusate Na − serum Na) ÷ (TBW + 1)

| Fluid | Na (mEq/L) | Free water per 1 L |
|---|---|---|
| **D5W / free water** | 0 | 1000 mL |
| 0.2% NaCl | 34 | ~780 mL |
| 0.45% NaCl | 77 | ~500 mL |
| LR | 130 | ~130 mL |
| 0.9% NaCl | 154 | **none** |
=== Worked example
**80-year-old woman, 50 kg, Na 162, from a nursing home. Chronic.**

- TBW = 50 × 0.45 = **22.5 L**
- FWD = 22.5 × (162 ÷ 140 − 1) = **~3.5 L** (total, over 2–3 days)
- 1 L D5W lowers Na by (0 − 162) ÷ 23.5 = **~7 mEq/L**
- Goal −10 in 24 h → **~1.5 L** D5W
- Add insensible loss **~1 L/day** (more with fever) + measured losses
- Start **D5W ~100 mL/h** (≈ 2.5 L/day). **Recheck Na in 4–6 h** and adjust.
=== Orders that work
- **Free-water flushes 250 mL q6h** via tube = 1 L/day. Write the **total daily free water** in the note.
- **D5W 100 mL/h** = 120 g glucose/day → **check glucose** (more glucose in urine = more water loss). Use enteral water when you can.
- **Can drink?** Water **at the bedside, within reach**. Ask nursing to **offer water every 2 h**.
- **Hypovolemic + high Na:** start LR/NS for volume, then switch to **0.45% NaCl** or D5W.
- **Hypervolemic (ICU, after NS/bicarb):** **D5W + loop diuretic**. A loop alone makes Na **worse** (loop urine is hypotonic).
=== DI treatment
**Central DI (AVP-D)**
- **Desmopressin 1–2 mcg IV/SC** q8–12h, or **0.1–0.2 mg PO** BID–TID.
- Give it **with Na checks**. Too much desmopressin + IV water → **hyponatremia**.
- After pituitary surgery: watch the **triple response** — DI → SIADH (~day 7) → DI again.

**Nephrogenic DI (AVP-R)**
- Fix the cause: correct **Ca and K**; stop lithium if possible (ask psychiatry).
- **Amiloride** for lithium-induced DI.
- **Thiazide** + **low-salt, low-protein diet** ± NSAID to lower urine volume.

**DI + intact thirst + free access to water** → Na usually stays normal. The danger is when they **cannot drink**.
:::

:::mnemonic Speed — "Ten a day keeps the edema away"
**Chronic:** ~**10 mEq/L per 24 h** (many use a ceiling of 12). **Acute:** **1 mEq/L per hour**. **Neither:** never let it **stall** above 150 for days.
:::

:::new What the data say about speed
- Brain swelling from fast correction is **well described in children**. In adults, observational data show it is **rare**.
- **Chauhan 2019** (ICU) and **Feigin 2023** (4265 adults with severe hypernatremia): faster correction (>0.5 mEq/L/h) was **not** linked to harm; **slow** correction was linked to **higher** mortality.
- **Kidney360 2025** (~38,000 adults): the opposite — slow correction had **lower** in-hospital mortality, but more discharges to hospice or a facility.
- **Bottom line:** all retrospective, no RCT. Aim for a **steady ~10 per day**, check often, and do not leave the patient salty for days.
:::

:::dose
Doses are typical adult doses. Check local protocols, glucose, and renal function. Free-water and D5W rates are a start — the **next Na** tells you the real dose.
:::

## 🏥 Real-life ward tips

- **Find the drain.** Lactulose with 6 stools/day, high-protein tube feeds (urea diuresis), post-obstructive diuresis, ATN recovery, fever, open wounds, and loop diuretics are the usual hidden drains.
- **Na still going up on D5W?** Check **urine volume and U-osm**. Calculate **electrolyte-free water clearance:** urine vol × [1 − (U-Na + U-K) ÷ serum Na]. Add this volume to your water plan.
- **Tube feeds contain water** (~70–85% water). Count it, but it is usually not enough alone.
- **HHS:** as glucose falls, **Na rises**. Follow the **corrected Na**. When corrected Na is normal or high, switch to **0.45% NaCl**.
- **Neuro ICU patient on 3% saline with Na goal 145–155?** This is on purpose. **Do not "fix" it** — talk to the team.
- **Discharge to a facility:** write it in the discharge orders: "**Offer water every 2 h while awake. Recheck BMP in 2–3 days.**"
- **Lithium patient with big urine output and Na 150?** Do not stop lithium alone without psychiatry. Check level, Ca, and kidney function.

## ⚠️ Pitfalls

- Using **NS as "water"** in a euvolemic patient — it has **zero** free water.
- Ordering the **free water once** and forgetting **ongoing losses** → Na does not move.
- Correcting **too slowly** — Na 158 on day 4 with "D5W at 50" is a common miss.
- D5W at a high rate in a diabetic → **hyperglycemia** → glycosuria → **more** water loss.
- **Desmopressin without Na checks** → overshoot into hyponatremia.
- Missing **lactulose** as the cause in a cirrhotic patient.
- Loop diuretic **alone** in hypervolemic hypernatremia.

| Stem | Answer |
|---|---|
| Nursing-home patient, fever, Na 160, U-osm 750 | Insensible/extra-renal loss + no access → free water |
| Polyuria after pituitary surgery, Na 152, U-osm 120 | Central DI (AVP-D) → desmopressin |
| U-osm 110 → 125 after desmopressin, on lithium | Nephrogenic DI (AVP-R) → amiloride |
| ICU, high-protein feeds, BUN 80, polyuria, U-osm 450 | Urea osmotic diuresis |
| Cirrhosis on lactulose, 8 stools/day, Na 155 | Osmotic GI water loss → free water, adjust lactulose |
| Hypotensive, dry, Na 158 | NS/LR for volume first, then free water |
