:::bottom
- **Normal 2.5–4.5 mg/dL.** Mild **2.0–2.4** · moderate **1.0–1.9** · **severe <1.0**.
- **Severe (<1.0) or symptoms** (weakness, failure to wean, rhabdo, hemolysis) → **IV phosphate**.
- **Mild–moderate, gut works → oral** phosphate. It is safer and works.
- **Pick the salt by the potassium:** K **<4** → **potassium phosphate**. K **≥4** or kidney failure → **sodium phosphate**.
- **Kidney failure (CrCl <30): half the dose**, then recheck.
- **Refeeding risk?** Thiamine first, feed slowly, check **phos/K/Mg every 12 h for 3 days**.
- Always check **Mg, K and Ca** at the same time — they travel together.
:::

## 🧠 Pathophysiology in 60 seconds

- **85%** of phosphate is in **bone**, most of the rest is **inside cells**. Only **<1%** is in the blood → the serum level is a **small window** on a big store.
- Phosphate is the "P" in **ATP** and in **2,3-DPG** (which helps red cells release O₂).
- **Kidney** controls the level: **PTH** and **FGF23** make the kidney **spill** phosphate. **Vitamin D (calcitriol)** helps the gut **absorb** it.
- Three ways to get low: phosphate **shifts into cells**, the kidney **spills** it, or **not enough comes in**.

{{fig:phos-battery}}

:::analogy
Phosphate is the **battery** of the cell. Low phos = **dead batteries**: the biggest power users fail first — the **diaphragm**, skeletal muscle, red cells, the brain and the heart.
:::

### Symptoms (usually only when <1.5, especially <1.0)

- **Muscle:** weakness, **respiratory failure / failure to wean**, **rhabdomyolysis**, ileus, dysphagia.
- **Heart:** low contractility, arrhythmias.
- **Blood:** **hemolysis**, poor O₂ release (low 2,3-DPG), WBC and platelet dysfunction.
- **Brain:** confusion, paresthesias, seizures, coma.
- **Chronic:** bone pain, **osteomalacia**, fractures.

## 🔎 Causes

{{fig:hypophos-3s}}

:::new IV iron can drop your phosphate
**Ferric carboxymaltose (Injectafer)** raises intact **FGF23** → the kidney spills phosphate. Hypophosphatemia in up to **~50–75%** of patients (vs **~8%** with ferric derisomaltose in PHOSPHARE). It can last **weeks** and cause osteomalacia with repeat doses. Ask about recent **IV iron** in any low phos without a clear cause.
:::

:::warn Refeeding syndrome — the dangerous one
- **Who:** little or no food for **>5–10 days**, BMI **<16–18.5**, weight loss **>10–15%**, **alcohol use disorder**, anorexia, cancer, post-bariatric, chronic diuretics or antacids.
- **What:** food → **insulin** → phosphate, K and Mg rush into cells → levels **crash** in **2–5 days** → heart failure, arrhythmia, respiratory failure, death.
- **ASPEN 2020:** a drop of phos, K or Mg by **10–20%** = mild, **20–30%** = moderate, **>30%** (or organ dysfunction) = severe — within **5 days** of feeding.
:::

## 🧪 Diagnosis

- **First ask: is there a shift?** Just started insulin, feeding, glucose, or hyperventilating? Then the kidney is probably fine.
- **Labs:** phos, **Mg, K, Ca, albumin**, creatinine, glucose, **CK** (rhabdo), LDH/haptoglobin (hemolysis), **25-OH vitamin D**, **PTH**.
- **Urine** (spot, **before** you replace): phos and creatinine → **FEPO₄**.

**FEPO₄ = (urine P × serum Cr) ÷ (serum P × urine Cr) × 100**

{{fig:hypophos-algo}}

:::pearl Units
**1 mmol phosphorus = 31 mg.** Serum **mg/dL × 0.323 = mmol/L.** Oral tablets are labeled in **mg**, IV bags in **mmol** — do not mix them up.
:::

## 💊 Management — easy replenishment guide

{{fig:phos-refill}}

:::flow
Q: Phos <1.0, OR symptoms (weakness, ventilator, rhabdo, hemolysis), OR cannot take by mouth?
Y: IV phosphate (table below). Recheck phos 2–4 h after the infusion ends or the next morning.
N: Oral phosphate 250–500 mg (8–16 mmol) TID. Recheck in 24 h.
Q: Potassium <4.0 and kidneys OK?
Y: Potassium phosphate (gives K too — watch the K rate).
N: Sodium phosphate.
Q: CrCl <30, AKI, or dialysis?
Y: Give 50% of the dose. Recheck before giving more.
Q: Calcium high, or Ca × P high?
Y: Slow down and talk to nephrology — risk of Ca-P precipitation.
:::

:::tabs
=== IV dose
**Step 1 — dose by level** (use actual weight; **adjusted** weight if obese). Many hospitals use the fixed doses on the right.

| Serum phos (mg/dL) | Weight-based | Fixed dose (≈70 kg) |
|---|---|---|
| **2.0–2.4** (mild) | 0.16 mmol/kg | **15 mmol** |
| **1.0–1.9** (moderate) | 0.32 mmol/kg | **30 mmol** |
| **<1.0** (severe) | 0.64 mmol/kg | **45 mmol** (max ~50 mmol/dose) |

**Step 2 — pick the salt:**

| Product | Per 1 mL | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Potassium phosphate** | 3 mmol P + **4.4 mEq K** | K <4.0, kidneys OK |
| **Sodium phosphate** | 3 mmol P + **4 mEq Na** | K ≥4.0, CKD/AKI, on K-sparing drugs |

**Step 3 — rate:** usually **≤7.5 mmol/h** (faster only in ICU per protocol).

| Order | Infuse over |
|---|---|
| NaPhos **15 mmol** | **2 h** |
| NaPhos **30 mmol** | **4 h** |
| KPhos **15 mmol** (22 mEq K) | **3 h** (K ≤10 mEq/h peripheral) |
| KPhos **30 mmol** (44 mEq K) | **6 h** |

**Step 4 — CrCl <30 / AKI / dialysis: half dose.** Recheck phos, K, Ca after.
=== Oral dose
**Mild–moderate (1.0–2.4) and gut works:** **250–500 mg (8–16 mmol) PO TID–QID** → about **1–2 g/day**.

| Product | Phosphorus | Na | K |
|---|---|---|---|
| **K-Phos Neutral** / Phospha 250 Neutral (tablet) | **250 mg (8 mmol)** | 13 mEq | 1.1 mEq |
| **Neutra-Phos** / Phos-NaK (powder packet) | **250 mg (8 mmol)** | 7.1 mEq | 7.1 mEq |
| **Neutra-Phos-K** (packet) | 250 mg (8 mmol) | 0 | 14.25 mEq |
| Skim **milk**, 1 cup (8 oz) | ~250 mg | — | — |

- Main side effect: **diarrhea** (dose-limiting). Split the dose, give with food.
- Give **2 h apart** from calcium, Mg/Al antacids, **sucralfate**, **sevelamer** and other binders — they bind it.
- **Neutra-Phos packet** = good when K is also low (refeeding, DKA recovery, alcohol use).
=== Example orders
- **Phos 2.1, K 4.3, eating:** K-Phos Neutral **1 tab (250 mg) PO TID × 3 doses** → BMP/phos in AM.
- **Phos 1.6, K 3.4, 70 kg, NPO:** **Potassium phosphate 30 mmol IV over 6 h** → phos, K in AM.
- **Phos 0.8, K 4.6, 80 kg, ventilated:** **Sodium phosphate 45 mmol IV over 6 h** → phos 2–4 h after. Repeat if still <1.0.
- **Phos 1.4, CrCl 22:** **Sodium phosphate 15 mmol IV over 2 h** (half of 30) → recheck before more.
- Many hospitals have a **nurse-driven electrolyte protocol** — use it when the patient qualifies.
=== Refeeding plan
- **Before feeding:** **thiamine 100–200 mg** daily (IV/PO) for 5–7 days; start before the first meal or dextrose.
- **Feed slowly:** start **10–20 kcal/kg/day** (5–10 if very high risk) → increase over **4–7 days**.
- **Labs:** phos, **K, Mg every 12 h for the first 3 days**, then daily.
- **Replace low levels before and during** feeding. Do not stop feeding for mild drops — slow it down.
- Watch **fluid** (edema, HF) and **telemetry** in high-risk patients.
=== Special cases
- **DKA:** phos falls with insulin — **do not replace routinely**. Replace if **<1.0 mg/dL**, or with cardiac dysfunction, hemolysis or respiratory depression (part of K replacement as KPhos).
- **CRRT:** effluent removes phosphate → use **phosphate-containing replacement fluid** or scheduled replacement.
- **Hungry bone** (after parathyroidectomy): Ca, phos and Mg all fall → calcium + calcitriol; replace phos carefully.
- **Chronic renal wasting** (X-linked hypophosphatemia, tumor-induced osteomalacia): oral phos + **calcitriol**; burosumab (anti-FGF23) for XLH. Endocrine.
:::

:::dose
Doses are typical adult doses. Check kidney function, Ca, K and your hospital electrolyte protocol. IV phosphate is for symptoms, severe lows or no gut — not for the convenience of one dose.
:::

## 🏥 Real-life ward tips

- **Patient fails the SBT for "no clear reason"?** Check **phos and Mg**. Weak diaphragm from low phos is easy to fix.
- **Draw the urine before you replace.** One spot urine P + Cr gives you the FEPO₄ and saves a big workup.
- **Low phos with low Mg** — fix the **Mg** too (Mg loss makes the kidney waste phosphate and K).
- **Alcohol withdrawal admission:** phos is often normal on day 1 and **crashes on day 2–3** when the patient eats and gets dextrose. Recheck.
- **Do not run IV phosphate and calcium in the same line** — they precipitate.
- **IV phos can drop the calcium** (Ca binds P). In a patient with low Ca, give oral phos or correct Ca first.
- **mg vs mmol errors:** "phosphorus 250" on a tablet = 8 mmol, not 250 mmol.
- **Mild low phos (2.0–2.4) and asymptomatic** in a patient who is eating? Often you can **just let them eat** (dairy, meat) and recheck — no pill needed.
- **Recent IV iron infusion clinic visit?** Think FGF23. Tell the iron clinic — switch product next time.

## ⚠️ Pitfalls

- Starting full-calorie feeds in a starved patient without checking phos/K/Mg.
- Giving **KPhos** to a patient with **K 5.2** or AKI.
- Full IV dose in **CrCl <30** → **hyperphosphatemia** and low Ca.
- Routine phos replacement in **DKA** — no benefit, more hypocalcemia.
- Giving oral phos **with** the calcium tablet or sevelamer.
- Forgetting **thiamine** before dextrose/feeds in alcohol use disorder.

## 🎓 Board corner

| Stem | Answer |
|---|---|
| Alcohol use disorder, day 3, eating, new weakness and hemolysis, phos 0.6 | Refeeding → IV phosphate |
| Anorexia nervosa, tube feeds started, 48 h later HF and phos 0.9 | Refeeding syndrome |
| Ventilated, failing SBT, phos 1.1 | Replete phos (diaphragm weakness) |
| DKA on insulin, phos 1.8, stable | No routine phos replacement |
| Low phos 3 weeks after ferric carboxymaltose, FEPO₄ 25% | FGF23-mediated renal wasting |
| Low phos + glucosuria (normal glucose) + aminoaciduria on tenofovir | Fanconi syndrome |
| Low phos, high PTH, low Ca, low 25-OH D | Vitamin D deficiency (secondary hyperPTH) |
| Low phos, high Ca, high PTH | Primary hyperparathyroidism |
| Hyperventilation/panic, phos 1.6 | Respiratory alkalosis → shift |
| FEPO₄ 2% | Kidney is saving it → GI loss, intake or shift |
| Child with rickets, low phos, high FGF23 | X-linked hypophosphatemia → burosumab |
